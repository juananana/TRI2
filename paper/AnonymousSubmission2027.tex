\title{Updating the World Is Not Rebinding the Target:\\
Temporal Referent Integrity in Tool-Using Agents}
\author{Anonymous Submission}
\affiliations{}

\begin{abstract}
Tool-using language agents repeatedly refresh external state while carrying out a user's
request. A refreshed observation, however, does not by itself authorize the agent to change
the entity denoted by an earlier reference. We formalize this post-binding authorization
problem as \emph{temporal referent integrity} (TRI): belief update and referent transition are
separate controller decisions. On a frozen 160-task minimal-pair diagnostic, a Generic
Structured Ledger that retains the instruction, selected ID, snapshot, selector, and action
preconditions reaches 64.4\% and 71.9\% with Qwen3.5-122B and GLM-5.1. Explicit pre-refresh
Compile-then-act reaches 95.0\% and 96.2\%, recovering most of the observed gap and supporting
pre-refresh commitment compilation as the leading mechanism. Deterministic Always-Lock and
Always-Reevaluate controls each reach only 60.0\% and fail opposite reference modes, showing
that the target is selective transition authorization rather than persistence alone. A separately
frozen 240-task core replication spans 40 new state clusters and ten unseen schemas. Conditional
on correct initial binding, Generic drifts on 43/72 Qwen and 38/80 GLM opportunities, versus zero
for Compile-then-act and Lifecycle-Gated; all 81 drifts become wrong-entity SQLite writes.
Compile-then-act improves replication accuracy by 23.3 and 24.2 points. A typed Lifecycle
representation reaches 98.1\% and 100.0\% on the primary set, but its gate adds only 1.2--1.9
points over a matched free actor and cannot repair compiler or selector errors. Three blind
annotators on 100 original/natural-rewrite items obtain Fleiss' $\kappa=.708$ and 86\%
majority-gold agreement. Public ToolSandbox/AppWorld audits identify natural post-binding
persistence traces but no native controlled TRI pair; ToolSandbox-style and AppWorld-backed
full-history studies observe zero conditional TRI, bounding universality. TRI therefore contributes a controlled, model- and controller-
conditional diagnosis and executable authorization contract, not a prevalence or safety claim.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Consider two requests issued before the same mailbox refresh:

\begin{quote}
\small
(1) ``Choose the highest-priority unread email now. Refresh the mailbox, then reply to it.''

(2) ``Refresh the mailbox first. Then choose the highest-priority unread email and reply.''
\end{quote}

Suppose email A is highest priority before refresh and email B afterward. The environment
transition is identical, but the correct target differs. Request (1) establishes A as an
identity commitment; request (2) deliberately leaves a description to be evaluated in the
new state. Acting on B in (1) is not stale-state correction. It is a wrong-entity action.

This distinction is easy to lose in LLM agent controllers. A controller may preserve the
entire transcript, append the latest observation, and ask a downstream model to act. All
facts remain available, yet the model can silently apply the old selector to the new state.
Conversely, always retaining an old ID breaks genuinely dynamic references. Reliable tool
use therefore needs \emph{selective invariance}: world facts should update while some target
denotations remain fixed, others are reevaluated, and invalid commitments follow an explicit
fallback policy.

We call this property \emph{temporal referent integrity} (TRI). TRI is not ordinary entity
memory. Remembering an ID does not say whether that ID is a committed referent or merely the
previous output of a dynamic selector. Nor does existence imply action validity: an email may
remain present but cease to be replyable. Finally, the selector that established identity is
not necessarily a persistent validity predicate. A branch chosen because it was default can
stop being default while remaining the correct branch on which to run checks.

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} summarizes the problem and our controller. A Generic Structured
Ledger records identity, facts, selector, and action preconditions, while leaving the actor to
infer how those fields interact across refresh. Minimal Compile-then-act (CTA) first records
binding time and identity. Its typed Lifecycle-Gated extension adds validity and fallback state;
a deterministic gate enforces preserved identities while an actor resolves dynamic branches.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formalize TRI as a post-binding authorization problem separating reference mode,
    bound identity, selector provenance, action-relative validity, and guarded fallback.
    Latest-state observations can be informationally insufficient; full history may contain
    the answer while still leaving the commitment computationally implicit and unenforced.
    \item We introduce frozen clustered diagnostics for language-template clusters,
    schema transfer under shared language templates, and guarded conditional commitments. We pre-specify cluster-resampled
    statistics and audit API failures, missing tasks, retries, and stage-wise errors.
    \item Across two models, independent human rewrites, SQLite mutations, and public trace
    audits, component
    evidence most strongly supports pre-refresh commitment compilation. Minimal CTA has the
    highest or tied scalar point estimate; typed lifecycle state exposes a richer authorization
    contract, while negative stress tests localize its compilation and compositional costs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Reference and entity binding.}
Discourse Representation Theory and centering distinguish discourse referents from changing
descriptions and salience \cite{kamp1993discourse,grosz1995centering}. Neural coreference
models and Quoref recover textual identity links \cite{lee2017coreference,joshi2020spanbert,
dasigi2019quoref}. Entity Binding Failures studies wrong initial tool arguments, provenance,
clarification, and confidence gating \cite{babu2026entitybinding}. These works ask whether an
entity was grounded correctly from the available context. Entity Binding Failures therefore asks
whether $bind(r,S_0)$ is correct; TRI conditions on a correct binding having occurred and asks
whether observing $S_1$ licenses a denotation transition, a question that arises only after the
binding event.

\paragraph{Binding persistence and drift.}
Binding Drift in multi-step tool-augmented agents is the closest neighboring formulation:
it studies a correctly selected entity being silently replaced during later workflow steps,
and evaluates entity locking and independent re-verification as countermeasures
\cite{indukuri2026bindingdrift}. TRI overlaps with its Preserve branch, but does not treat every
post-binding target change as an error. The same transition is correct to preserve for
``choose now, refresh, then act on it'' and correct to reevaluate for ``refresh first, then
choose.'' Thus an unconditional lock and an unconditional re-verification policy are both
necessarily wrong on one half of TRI minimal pairs. Our deterministic controls quantify these
two extremes on the frozen inventory; they are conceptual policy controls, not a claim to
reproduce the full Binding Drift agent or its workflow benchmark. The distinction is therefore
authorization of a denotation transition, rather than target persistence alone.

\paragraph{Entity tracking and agent memory.}
Recent entity-tracking work tests whether language models preserve entity identity while facts
change across state transitions \cite{tang2026entitytracking}. Memory systems retain summaries,
experiences, skills, or retrieved traces, including Generative Agents, Reflexion, Self-Refine,
Voyager, MemGPT, MemoryBank, and AutoGen \cite{park2023generative,shinn2023reflexion,
madaan2023selfrefine,wang2023voyager,packer2023memgpt,zhong2024memorybank,wu2023autogen};
surveys and cognitive-architecture work organize these choices
\cite{zhang2024memorysurvey,sumers2024coala}. ReAct interleaves reasoning and acting
\cite{yao2023react}. TRI isolates a missing relationship in such state: whether a selector
remains executable after refresh or has become provenance for a committed identity.

\paragraph{Tool-use and interactive benchmarks.}
Toolformer, API-Bank, ToolLLM, and Gorilla study API acquisition and tool calling
\cite{schick2023toolformer,li2023apibank,qin2024toolllm,patil2023gorilla}. AgentBench,
WebArena, VisualWebArena, WebShop, GAIA, SWE-bench, and MINT evaluate agents in web, coding,
multimodal, and feedback-rich environments \cite{liu2024agentbench,zhou2024webarena,
koh2024visualwebarena,yao2022webshop,mialon2024gaia,jimenez2024swebench,wang2024mint}.
ToolSandbox, AppWorld, and $\tau$-bench add stateful applications, policies, and user
interaction \cite{lu2025toolsandbox,trivedi2024appworld,yao2024taubench}. $\tau^2$-bench
introduces dual-control settings in which user-side and agent-side actions can affect a shared
environment; the current public repository has progressed to a $\tau^3$-bench release with
additional domains and modalities \cite{barres2025tau2,sierra2026tau3}. These benchmarks are
strong candidates for external validation, but their default task gold does not explicitly
label post-binding Preserve versus Reevaluate transitions, so a TRI study must freeze a
transparent adaptation rather than reuse a leaderboard score as TRI evidence. ToolEmu studies
agent risk \cite{ruan2024toolemu}. Dynamic-environment suites such as ClawArena and EvoArena
focus on evolving information and memory robustness \cite{ji2026clawarena,xu2026evoarena}.
Temporal Blindness and TicToc instead study whether elapsed time should trigger a tool refresh
\cite{cheng2026temporalblindness}; TRI conditions on an update having occurred and asks whether
that update authorizes changing an already established action referent.
TRI complements these benchmarks with controlled temporal-target minimal pairs and a focused
SQLite mutation test rather than claiming coverage of their broader environments.

\paragraph{Executable constraints and diagnostic evaluation.}
LMQL, guided generation, and DSPy structure model calls or compile declarative programs
\cite{beurer2023lmql,willard2023guided,khattab2024dspy}. LedgerAgent combines typed state with
policy checks. Bounded Autonomy constrains mutations with typed consumer-side action contracts,
but also reports that wrong-entity mutations can escape schema- and policy-derived checks
without separate disambiguation or confirmation \cite{uddin2026ledgeragent,sohail2026bounded}.
These checks ask whether a proposed action is permitted for its proposed entity; they do not by
themselves determine which entity the discourse authorized. TRI instead asks the earlier
discourse-derived question: whether selection in $S_0$ established
an identity commitment before refresh, or left a description to be evaluated in $S_1$.
Execution-time validity remains necessary but does not authorize substituting a different valid
entity. We do not claim the first ledger, typed contract, or gate. Finally, CheckList and contrast sets motivate our
clustered minimal-pair evaluation \cite{ribeiro2020checklist,gardner2020contrast}.

\section{Problem Formulation: Temporal Referent Integrity}

Let $u$ be an instruction, $r$ its action-target reference, $S_0$ the state before refresh,
$S_1$ the refreshed state, and $a$ the requested action. We factor the executable contract into
a referential lifecycle $R$ and an action policy $P$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
L(r,a)&=(R(r),P(a)),\\
R&=(m,e,q,g),\qquad P=(V_a,\pi),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is a reference mode, $e$ is an identity bound in $S_0$, $q$ is a selector,
$g$ is an optional persistence guard, $\pi$ is a fallback policy, and $V_a(e,S)$ is validity
under the requested action schema. The main TRI-v3 setting uses
$m\in\{\textsc{Preserve},\textsc{Reevaluate}\}$ and an explicit reject policy for an invalid
preserved target. TRI-v4 adds conditional guards.

For the scalar policies evaluated here, the execution branches are
\begin{align}
\textsc{Preserve},\;V_a(e,S_1) &\Rightarrow e, \\
\textsc{Preserve},\;\neg V_a(e,S_1),\;\pi=\textsc{Reject} &\Rightarrow \bot, \\
\textsc{Reevaluate} &\Rightarrow q(S_1), \\
\textsc{Conditional},\;g(e,q,S_1) &\Rightarrow e, \\
\textsc{Conditional},\;\neg g(e,q,S_1) &\Rightarrow q(S_1).
\end{align}
An invalid preserved entity remains the discourse referent $e$ under $R$ even when $P$ makes
the authorized action output $\bot$. Thus rejection is an execution-policy decision, not a
claim that the reference denotes nothing. The human study below empirically motivates this
factorization: referential transitions receive stronger agreement than invalid-target handling.
TRI-v4 distinguishes $g_{\mathrm{act}}=V_a(e,S_1)$ from
$g_{\mathrm{sel}}=[q(S_1)=e]$. This matters when a refresh changes the selector winner but
the original entity remains action-valid: $g_{\mathrm{act}}$ preserves it, whereas
$g_{\mathrm{sel}}$ rebinds.

Three insufficiency pairs motivate this factorization. First, two tasks can share $S_1$, $q$,
and $a$ but differ only in whether the discourse selected before or after refresh. Latest-state
control cannot recover the missing commitment. Second, two tasks can store the same old ID,
but in one it is committed and in the other it is merely a stale dynamic-selector result;
an ID without a role annotation cannot choose correctly for both. Third, two tasks can share mode and ID while
requesting actions with different preconditions. The target may be valid for viewing but not
for canceling, shipping, or approving. Validity is therefore $V_a(e,S)$, not mere existence.

These are three distinct claims. An observation may be \emph{informationally insufficient}
because a binding event was discarded. A full transcript or ledger may be informationally
sufficient but leave the role of an ID \emph{computationally implicit} in prose. Even a model
that infers the intended role correctly does not provide \emph{enforcement}: a later actor can
still violate that decision unless the mutation boundary checks it. TRI targets the latter two
failure modes without claiming that full history is unable to represent the answer.

The selector $q$ and validity $V_a$ have distinct roles. In a preserved reference, $q$ was
used to establish identity in $S_0$; reapplying it after refresh is precisely the error under
study. Only the action schema determines whether the committed entity can still receive $a$.
Conditional language can explicitly reactivate $q$, but that permission must be represented
rather than inferred afresh by every downstream actor.

The contract makes the authorization boundary explicit. Let $\mathcal{A}(L,S_1)$ be the
set of targets licensed after refresh:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}(L,S_1)=
\begin{array}{ll}
\{e\}, & \substack{m=\textsc{Preserve},\\ V_a(e,S_1)},\\
\{q(S_1)\}, & m=\textsc{Reevaluate},\\
\{e\}, & \substack{m=\textsc{Conditional},\\ g(e,q,S_1)},\\
\{q(S_1)\}, & \substack{m=\textsc{Conditional},\\ \neg g(e,q,S_1)},\\
\{q(S_1)\}, & \substack{m=\textsc{Preserve},\\ \neg V_a(e,S_1),\ \pi=\textsc{Reevaluate}},\\
\{\bot\}, & \substack{m=\textsc{Preserve}\land\neg V_a(e,S_1),\\
\pi=\textsc{Reject}.}
\end{array}
\end{equation}
TRI compliance requires a proposed target $x$ to belong to $\mathcal{A}(L,S_1)$. Mutation
authorization is a second check: a non-rejection proposal may execute only if
$V_a(x,S_1)$. If it fails, the mutation boundary blocks it; action validity alone never
authorizes substituting a different valid target. Whether the blocked outcome is surfaced as
rejection, clarification, or another explicit fallback belongs to $P$, not to referential
semantics. This two-layer condition applies only after the controller has compiled the correct
mode, identity, guard, and fallback from the instruction; it does not assume an infallible LLM
compiler.

\paragraph{Proposition (mode-blind policy impossibility).}
Consider a Preserve/Reevaluate minimal pair with identical $S_0$, $S_1$, $q$, and $a$, where
$e=q(S_0)$, $e'=q(S_1)$, and $e\ne e'$. The authorized targets are $e$ and $e'$, respectively.
Any deterministic post-refresh policy of the form $f(e,q,S_1,a)$, containing neither the
original discourse nor a compiled mode (or equivalent discourse-sensitive distinction),
receives the same inputs for both tasks and must return the same target; it is therefore wrong
on at least one member. Always-Lock and
Always-Reevaluate instantiate the two complementary extremes measured in our policy controls.
This establishes the need for a selective transition decision, not the necessity of our
particular lifecycle serialization.

\paragraph{Observation (state-overwrite indistinguishability).}
Consider a controller whose post-refresh state is exactly $(u,S_1)$, where $u$ is the
original instruction and $S_1$ is the refreshed observation. There exist two histories with
the same anchored instruction $u$ and the same $S_1$, but different initial states $S_0,S'_0$
such that $bind(r,S_0)=e$, $bind(r,S'_0)=e'$, and $e\ne e'$. If both histories refresh to
$S_1$, the controller receives the same post-refresh input while the correct targets differ.
Therefore any deterministic state-overwrite controller is wrong on at least one history.
This matches the tested baseline: the temporal instruction remains in the input, but the
earlier binding result is not recoverable from the common latest state. The construction is a
diagnostic lower bound, not a claim that every full-history or learned-memory controller fails.

\paragraph{Authorization invariant (conditional guarantee).}
Assume (i) $L$ correctly encodes the user's authorized mode, identity, selector, guard, and
fallback; (ii) entity IDs remain stable across the evaluated transition; (iii) selector and
guard evaluation are correct and $V_a$ completely implements the action schema; and (iv) the executor follows the branches
defining $\mathcal{A}(L,S_1)$. Then every proposed non-rejection target belongs to
$\mathcal{A}(L,S_1)$. If the mutation tool executes only proposals satisfying $V_a$, every
executed mutation is both referentially authorized and action-valid; an authorized but invalid
proposal is blocked rather than silently replaced. The first claim follows by cases on $m$,
$g$, and $\pi$, and the second by the mutation precondition. The guarantee ends at the compiled
contract: a wrong but action-valid bound ID can still pass, and identity migration or an
incomplete action schema violates the assumptions.

\paragraph{Safety-sensitive outcomes.}
Exact target accuracy treats every mismatch equally, but the operational consequences differ.
A \emph{wrong-target attempt} names an entity other than the gold target; if that entity is
present and action-valid, it becomes a \emph{wrong-entity write}. An \emph{invalid-target
attempt} names an entity that violates action preconditions and should be blocked by the tool.
An \emph{unnecessary rejection} returns $\bot$ when a valid target exists. The first two can
modify the wrong external record; the last is conservative loss of utility. We therefore report
resolution accuracy, final database state, wrong writes, invalid attempts, and unnecessary
rejections separately. TRI does not define safety as refusal: a controller that always rejects
would avoid wrong writes but fail every actionable task.

\section{Method: Pre-Refresh Commitment Compilation}

The controller family first compiles $(u,S_0)$ before refresh. Minimal CTA emits binding time,
selector, and a bound ID. The typed Lifecycle extension additionally emits
\begin{quote}
\small\ttfamily
reference\_mode; bound\_target\_id; selector; invalidity\_policy.
\end{quote}
Both compilers use discourse order: ``identify, refresh, act on it'' preserves identity even
without words such as ``same,'' while ``refresh before deciding'' reevaluates. It resolves a
concrete bound ID only for preserved references.

The executor then refreshes the environment. If the compiled mode is \textsc{Preserve}, a
deterministic gate checks the bound entity against action preconditions. The gate emits the
bound ID when valid, returns $\bot$ under a reject policy, or invokes selector resolution only
when the compiled fallback authorizes it. If the mode is \textsc{Reevaluate}, the LLM actor
selects from $S_1$. Thus a preserved commitment is a controller constraint, not advice that a
second model may reinterpret. The preserve branch also needs one model call rather than two.

TRI-v4 generalizes the record with \texttt{guard\_type} and
\texttt{fallback\_policy}. The action-validity guard can be enforced deterministically. A
selector-match guard is evaluated by the actor because the current prototype compiles natural
language selectors but does not execute them as a general symbolic query language. This design
choice exposes selector grounding as a measurable stage rather than using gold selector fields.

The complete controller procedure and model-facing interfaces are provided in the supplement.
Gold targets and benchmark selector fields are never provided to the compiler or actor.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Controlled task construction.}
Each domain specification defines an entity table, a natural-language selector, its ranking or
Boolean criterion, an action, and explicit action preconditions. The generator first evaluates
the selector on $S_0$ to obtain $e_0$, applies a controlled transition, and evaluates it on
$S_1$ to obtain $e_1$. A language template determines whether the action target is $e_0$,
$e_1$, $\bot$, or a conditionally guarded choice. Paired tasks share the domain, both states,
selector, action, and schema; only discourse order and anaphoric language change. This prevents
domain difficulty or target frequency from explaining the preserve/reevaluate contrast.

The five transition types serve different diagnostic roles. \emph{Flip} changes which valid
entity wins the selector. \emph{Stable} leaves the winner unchanged and detects controllers
that overreact to any refresh. \emph{Remove} deletes the pre-refresh entity. \emph{Invalidate}
keeps its ID present while changing an action-precondition field, separating existence from
validity. \emph{Name collision} gives another entity the same display label while changing the
selector winner, testing whether controllers preserve stable identity rather than surface
aliases. Across anchored and dynamic language, these updates create matched cases where naive
``always old'' and ``always new'' policies have complementary failures.

\paragraph{Blind human construct validation.}
One independent volunteer naturally rewrote 50 sampled scalar instructions. Three other
volunteers, uninvolved in TRI design, independently labeled 100 randomized original/rewrite
items using opaque IDs without condition metadata or gold answers. They selected an entity,
\textsc{Reject}, or \textsc{Clarify}; confidence was optional. We report majority--gold
alignment, unanimous and pairwise agreement, Fleiss' $\kappa$, nominal Krippendorff's $\alpha$,
and a consensus-only sensitivity analysis. Conditional, collection, and nested cases were not
included in this human study.

Stage and write-consequence reports use a frozen mechanism-oriented error taxonomy, detailed
in the supplement, that separates temporal rebinding, premature binding, invalid processing,
unnecessary rejection, and selector-grounding errors.

Table~\ref{tab:components} reports the frozen v3 primary inventory. Generic validity gating,

were post-primary audits; they do not replace the pre-specified Generic-versus-Lifecycle-Gated
estimand. They do, however, determine how narrowly that comparison can be interpreted.

\paragraph{TRI-v3 language clusters.}
The primary test contains 160 tasks formed from 20 frozen language-template clusters,
four reference styles, and eight domains per template. Within each style, flip, stable, remove,
invalidate, and name-collision updates are balanced. The pre-specified statistic resamples
whole template clusters with 10,000 bootstrap samples; task-level McNemar is secondary. Before
any model call, we froze the data, runner, hashes, inference settings, and rule that prompts
would not change after output inspection.

\paragraph{Schema transfer under shared language templates and guarded policies.}
An 80-task transfer set crosses the same 20 template clusters with four new domains:
projects, expenses, inventory, and cloud deployments. These introduce new IDs, fields,
selectors, actions, and action preconditions. TRI-v4 separately freezes 40 conditional tasks,
ten template clusters, and the two guards $g_{\mathrm{act}}$ and $g_{\mathrm{sel}}$. Every
guard--update--domain combination occurs once. TRI-v4 is a secondary extension, not part of
the pre-specified v3 primary comparison.

\paragraph{Direct-neighbor baseline.}
Generic Structured Ledger uses two calls. Its compiler stores the initial selected ID, complete
entity snapshot, selector, requested action, and action preconditions, but omits reference mode
and invalidity policy. Its actor still receives the original instruction, ledger, $S_1$, and
action schema. It is therefore not an information-deletion baseline: the comparison asks
whether structured facts suffice without discourse commitment semantics.

The historical Compile-then-act prompt is rerun unchanged on the v3 language set
(Table~\ref{tab:components}). Its cluster interval overlaps lifecycle-free execution for both
models, consistent with pre-refresh compilation, rather than tuple serialization or deterministic
gating alone, being the leading explanation. Because it emits binding time and a bound ID,
it also serves as the simple mode-classifier-plus-ID-lock analogue for scalar cases. The typed contract contributes an auditable
interface for validity and fallback; the gate is a small enforcement layer, not the principal
accuracy mechanism. A matched free-form pre-refresh planner reaches only 81.2\% for Qwen and
70.6\% for GLM, so moving computation before refresh is not alone sufficient; nevertheless,
the strong historical predecessor prevents us from claiming that the full tuple is empirically
necessary for scalar tasks.

For a direct policy contrast, we also evaluate two deterministic extremes without model calls.
Always-Lock + validity retains the $S_0$ ID and rejects it only when the refreshed action
preconditions fail; Always-Reevaluate discards the binding and applies $q$ to $S_1$. These are
conceptual controls rather than implementations of the Binding Drift baselines. On the frozen
160-task inventory both reach 96/160 (60.0\%), but their mode slices are complementary:
Always-Lock reaches 80/80 anchored and 16/80 dynamic, whereas Always-Reevaluate reaches
16/80 anchored and 80/80 dynamic. The contrast is a diagnostic of selective authorization,
not evidence that either unconditional policy is a competitive agent controller.

\paragraph{Model-facing SQLite trajectories.}
We freeze a 40-task subset covering all 20 templates, eight domains, both modes, and all update
types. The runtime queries an in-memory SQLite database, gives returned rows to the compiler,
replaces the database state during refresh, obtains a target from the gate or actor, and executes
\texttt{UPDATE} through a mutation tool that enforces action preconditions. We inspect final
database state, wrong writes, invalid attempts, and collateral modifications. Query and required
refresh order are controller-orchestrated; this is a focused execution test, not a fully
autonomous external benchmark.

\paragraph{Models, inference, and auditing.}
We use Qwen3.5-122B-A10B and GLM-5.1 through the same OpenAI-compatible API at temperature zero,
with thinking disabled and a 1,200-token output cap. Balanced smoke tests gate each expansion.
Final reports verify expected task IDs, duplicates, status, request attempts, and retries. All
reported v3/v4 full files are complete and contain zero API errors and zero retries. API failures
would be reported separately rather than interpreted as model behavior.

\paragraph{Statistical estimands.}
The primary estimand is Lifecycle-Gated minus Generic Structured Ledger accuracy on the Qwen
language-cluster set. We resample its 20 complete template clusters rather than treating 160
templated rows as independent. The same cluster bootstrap is reported for replication and
transfer sets; each interval uses 10,000 samples and a fixed recorded seed. Template-macro and
task accuracy coincide in the balanced sets, but both are retained in machine-readable reports.
Exact paired McNemar tests use task-level discordance and are secondary. Wilson intervals
describe individual proportions. Slices by binding, explicitness, update, and domain are
mechanism analyses rather than additional confirmatory hypotheses; we do not use them to select
the primary claim.

\paragraph{Reproducible run discipline.}
Frozen data, protocols, runners, and smoke subsets have recorded SHA-256 hashes. Reports retain
raw outputs, attempts, retries, stage errors, and SQLite traces; interrupted rows are auditable by
ID. The anonymous artifact maps every main-table value to a raw run and report script, includes
the human-analysis code and de-identified responses, and excludes API keys and private
coordination files. Post-primary audits are labeled as such.

\paragraph{Structured facts are not a commitment.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:results} show the same mechanism across models.
On the primary language-cluster test, Generic Structured Ledger reaches 64.4\% for Qwen and
71.9\% for GLM, despite carrying identity, facts, selector, preconditions, and the original
instruction. Lifecycle-Gated reaches 98.1\% and 100.0\%. Paired discordance is 3 generic-only
versus 57 lifecycle-only for Qwen ($p=6.25\times10^{-14}$), and 0 versus 45 for GLM
($p=5.68\times10^{-14}$). Qwen improves from 33.8\% to 100.0\% on anchored references while
remaining comparable on dynamic references (95.0\% versus 96.2\%). GLM improves from 56.2\%
to 100.0\% anchored and from 87.5\% to 100.0\% dynamic. The gain is therefore not explained by
always preserving old IDs.

Because the Lifecycle compiler receives explicit reference-mode and invalidity semantics, this
comparison does not by itself identify the causal contribution of serialization, prompt
supervision, and the deterministic gate separately. The historical compile-then-act results in
Table~\ref{tab:components} provide a strong neighboring comparison and motivate the narrower
interpretation: explicit pre-refresh compilation is the main performance mechanism, while
lifecycle state makes its authorization and validity decisions auditable and enforceable.

The post-primary component audit sharpens this conclusion. A validity-only gate changes
Generic accuracy by only +0.6 points for Qwen and +1.2 for GLM. Lifecycle state with a free
actor reaches 96.9\% and 98.1\%; deterministic enforcement adds only +1.2 and +1.9 points.
The new Generic+reference-mode ablation reaches 75.0\% for both models, with paired cluster
differences of +10.6 $[+5.0,+16.9]$ and +3.1 $[-2.5,+9.4]$ points. Thus explicit mode
classification is useful but model-dependent and insufficient to explain CTA's full gain.
The call-matched untyped plan reaches 81.2\% and 70.6\%, 15.6 and 27.5 points below
Lifecycle-free with cluster intervals excluding zero, showing that pre-refresh timing alone is
insufficient. Exact historical CTA reaches 95.0\% and 96.2\%, tied with Lifecycle-free; the
audit therefore supports commitment compilation as the main mechanism, with typed lifecycle
state adding auditable validity and fallback rather than explaining the full accuracy gap.

The deterministic policy controls further separate TRI from unconditional persistence.
Always-Lock + validity and Always-Reevaluate each reach only 60.0\% overall. Their equal totals
hide opposite errors: lock is 100.0\% on anchored and 20.0\% on dynamic references, while
reevaluation is 20.0\% anchored and 100.0\% dynamic. TRI's empirical target is the discourse-
conditioned choice between these transitions, not locking by default.

Because the aggregate includes a normative invalid-target policy, we also separate its 128
actionable core items: Generic/Historical-CTA/Gated reach 74.2/98.4/97.7\% for Qwen and
72.7/99.2/100.0\% for GLM. The complete 32-item policy slice is supplementary; this
post-primary sensitivity does not replace pre-specified total accuracy.

The strongest generic failures occur when the bound entity remains valid but no longer wins
the selector: GLM obtains 18.8\% on anchored flips and 25.0\% on name collisions, while the
lifecycle gate is perfect. Implicit commitments are also harder for generic state (42.5\%
anchored for GLM) than explicit ones (70.0\%). A field-corrected stage audit is important here:
Generic stores the initial target as \texttt{selected\_entity\_id}, not the lifecycle-only
\texttt{bound\_target\_id}. Conditioning on the former being correct, Qwen drifts to the refreshed
leader on 15/16 anchored flips and 14/16 name collisions; GLM does so on 3/16 and 7/16,
respectively, while stable controls show 0 drift. These patterns match the predicted distinction
between storing evidence and enforcing what that evidence means for denotation.

\paragraph{A larger frozen replication confirms conditional drift.}
After selecting the mechanism, we froze 240 core-only tasks over ten unseen schemas and four
independently parameterized states per schema. All old targets remain present and action-valid;
the 40 state instances are the resampling units. Generic binds 107/120 Qwen and 120/120 GLM
anchored targets correctly. Within the resulting flip/name-collision opportunities it then drifts
to the refreshed winner 43/72 times (59.7\%, state-cluster 95\% CI [46.5,72.4]) and 38/80
(47.5\%, [35.0,61.3]); stable final-target errors are 4/40 and 2/40. Exact CTA and Gated have
zero such drift after a correct binding. Overall CTA improves over Generic by 23.3 points
[16.2,30.4] and 24.2 [19.6,28.7]. Qwen's lower dynamic accuracy exposes compound-selector
grounding rather than temporal drift, so this post-primary replication strengthens the existence
claim without converting the synthetic benchmark into a prevalence estimate.

\paragraph{Human judgments support the core transition, with a fallback boundary.}
Across 100 blinded items, Fleiss' $\kappa=.708$, Krippendorff's $\alpha=.709$, and majority--gold
agreement is 86\% (91.5\% among 94 determinate items); original and rewritten language obtain
84\% and 88\%. On 50 frozen volunteer rewrites, Generic/CTA/Lifecycle-free/Gated reach
60/90/88/90\% for Qwen and 74/98/92/94\% for GLM. Direct CTA-minus-Gated intervals are 0.0
$[-11.1,+11.1]$ and +4.0 $[-4.3,+14.8]$ points; CTA-minus-Lifecycle-free intervals also span
zero. Against 48 rewrite majorities, CTA reaches 89.6/93.8\% versus Gated's 85.4/85.4\%; on 41
majority-actionable items, the values are 92.7/92.7\% versus 82.9/82.9\%. Lifecycle errors
mostly miscompile refresh-first rewrites as \textsc{Preserve}; CTA errors concentrate in invalid-target
policy. Action-invalid \textsc{Reject} obtains only 55\% majority--gold and 25\% unanimity, so
we treat Preserve/Reevaluate as the human-supported core and Reject as a normative action policy.

\subsection{External and Compositional Boundaries}
The external and compositional evaluations do not show a stable extension of the scalar result.
In the 24-task ToolSandbox-style pilot, Generic, Lifecycle, Gated, and Untyped obtain
79.2/87.5/91.7/91.7\% for GLM and 91.7/83.3/91.7/79.2\% for Qwen, respectively. Gated
permits one GLM and two Qwen wrong writes. We therefore treat this pilot as an external
validity boundary check: the TRI diagnosis can be instantiated in a stateful tool loop, but
the controller advantage is not stable once tool choice and upstream compilation are exposed.

We additionally froze a 96-task, single-user-turn ToolSandbox-based 2$\times$2 extension with an
auditable binding opportunity. Qwen and GLM full-history agents produced zero TRI mechanism
errors in 70 and 73 eligible opportunities, respectively; matched Qwen and GLM Generic Ledgers
produced zero in 64 and 87. The external runs did produce real wrong-entity writes (6, 13, 5,
and 4), but each was
an initial selector or tool-order error outside the TRI denominator. We report this negative
external result as a boundary, not as prevalence evidence: the controlled Generic result is
model- and controller-dependent and does not imply that every full-history LLM will exhibit TRI.

We also audited unmodified public inventories before adaptation. At pinned ToolSandbox commit
\texttt{165848b}, none of 129 semantic scenario families contains a strict post-binding external
transition plus a later substitutable mutation; one task naturally preserves ``them'' after the
selected contacts change category. Across AppWorld's 244 generator families, one public Todoist
family is a stronger natural near-match: tasks selected as assigned-to-me are reassigned and then
``leave a comment there'' must retain the same task IDs even though they no longer satisfy the
initial selector. In 42 released trajectories from 14 experiment configurations, 16 correct
target-binding operations are followed by 16 same-ID comments and zero post-binding substitutions;
most failures omit targets before binding. Thus an independent benchmark supports the task
structure, but neither inventory estimates strict TRI prevalence. At current $	au^3$-bench
commit \texttt{cf71a80}, a separate audit covers 2,449 airline/retail/telecom tasks and 10,832
released GPT-4.1/Claude/o4-mini trajectories. It finds zero strict same-role TRI opportunities;
eight telecom tasks naturally bind a bill, let the user pay, and continue on an associated line,
but bill and line are different roles with no competing selector target.

Finally, a custom AppWorld case study crosses reference mode, Stable/Flip, and two paraphrases in
two application/selector clusters: Todoist earliest-due tasks (frozen first) and a post-primary
Simple Note alphabetical-title extension. Across 32 Qwen/GLM full-history trajectories using
native APIs and database IDs, 31 write the authorized target. Of 24 trajectories with a correct,
correctly timed observable binding, conditional TRI is 0/24 (0/4 in Preserve/Flip). The sole
wrong write follows synchronization before binding and is therefore a delayed-binding/tool-order
error, not post-binding drift. This is a custom adaptation, not an AppWorld leaderboard score,
but it confirms that the external null is not an artifact of label-only scoring.

A lower-intervention addendum then removes the binding sidecar and all prompt language about
TRI, temporal authorization, or reference modes. Ordinary selector APIs expose the returned ID
without a measurement call. Across the same two apps and models, 28/32 trajectories form a
correctly timed binding and conditional TRI is 0/28 (0/6 Preserve/Flip). Qwen makes two real
Flip wrong writes, but in each matched app it synchronizes before its first selector call; the
corresponding Stable row has the same order defect but masks it because the winner is unchanged.
Thus these are pre-binding temporal-order errors, not post-binding drift.

The frozen Qwen TRI-v5 stress test contains two refreshes, a monitoring referent, an unrelated
tool call, and a final mutation. Generic obtains 32/40 final states, one wrong write, and four
unnecessary rejections; scalar Lifecycle obtains 28/40, six wrong writes, six unnecessary
rejections, and one parse/internal error. The difference is $-10.0$ points with a
template-cluster interval of $[-35.0,12.5]$. A role-indexed held-out addendum improves Qwen
from 35/40 to 39/40 final states, but recovered GLM is tied at 40/40; this motivates role
indexing without establishing stable superiority over simpler compilation. These results
bound the current claim to controller-orchestrated, single-refresh, scalar mutations rather
than general multi-referent reliability.

\subsection{Error and Safety Analysis}
\paragraph{Stage analysis localizes the remaining error.}
On language clusters, GLM compiles mode, ID, and policy correctly on all tasks and has no actor
failure. Qwen also compiles all lifecycle fields correctly; its three final errors occur in the
dynamic actor. Under schema transfer with shared templates, mode remains 100\%, but bound-ID accuracy falls to 55.0\%
for explicit and 50.0\% for implicit anchored references. The errors concentrate in compound
selectors such as ``lowest-stock reorderable item'' and ``oldest failing deployment'': Qwen
sometimes chooses the lowest item before filtering \texttt{reorderable}, or the oldest
deployment before filtering \texttt{failing}. The gate then rejects the ineligible ID. This
produces conservative errors rather than wrong writes, but it shows that TRI semantics and
selector grounding are separate research problems.

\paragraph{Transfer and real write consequences.}
On the four new schemas under shared templates, lifecycle control improves Qwen from 46.2\% to 82.5\%; the
cluster interval excludes zero. In model-facing SQLite runs, Generic Structured Ledger gives Qwen 27/40 final states with 13
wrong-entity writes, while GLM gives 26/40 with 8 wrong-entity writes. A conditional trace audit
separates the referential core from invalidity policy. Both models compile the correct initial ID
on all 20 anchored tasks. Among the eight flip/name-collision cases in which that entity remains
present and action-valid, Generic writes the refreshed selector winner in 8/8 Qwen and 6/8 GLM
trajectories; stable controls have 0/4 wrong writes for each model. The remaining 5 and 2 wrong
writes occur after removal or invalidation and therefore test the benchmark's reject policy
rather than the core post-binding transition. Lifecycle-
Gated reaches 40/40 for both models with no wrong write, invalid attempt, unnecessary
rejection, or collateral modification. The paired exact test for Qwen gives
$p=2.44\times10^{-4}$; the GLM paired cluster difference is reported in Table~\ref{tab:main}.
These are actual post-refresh database mutations, not target-string labels. Generic plus a
validity-only gate still produces 13 Qwen wrong writes because replacement targets are often
action-valid; both Lifecycle variants obtain 40/40. This demonstrates a correctly compiled
contract, not general reliability against compiler or tool failures.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Commitments benefit from explicit controller state.}
Generic already carries enough evidence in principle; its failures instead show that an implicit
relationship remains behaviorally unstable: is a selector still an instruction to search, or
provenance for a committed ID? Pre-refresh compilation answers this once. Typed lifecycle state
makes the answer auditable and separates revisable beliefs from discourse commitments without
claiming that the tuple is the only sufficient representation.

\paragraph{Gates move, rather than erase, the reliability bottleneck.}
A gate cannot repair a wrong mode, ID, or guard; an incorrectly bound yet valid ID can pass.
It only prevents a later actor from violating a correctly compiled contract and enforces action
validity. Under schema transfer this sometimes converts grounding errors into rejection, shifting
rather than eliminating risk and motivating clarification and executable selectors. In the new
replication's SQLite replay, CTA/Gated produce no core TRI write after a correct binding, yet
Qwen still makes 8/17 and GLM 14/7 total wrong writes, mainly from dynamic over-locking or
selector errors. Enforcement is therefore conditional, not a general write-safety guarantee.

\paragraph{Boundary results constrain the design claim.}
The ToolSandbox and multi-referent results belong to the empirical finding, not merely to future
work: a correctly designed gate cannot compensate for an incorrectly compiled contract or an
upstream tool error, and a scalar contract does not compose automatically across referent roles.
Within the supported scalar setting, gating reduces primary requests from 320 to 240 per model.

\section{Limitations}

\paragraph{Scope and construct validity.}
TRI is a controlled diagnostic, not a prevalence estimate. It still simplifies ambiguity,
repair, partial observability, and identity migration. Human validation covers 100 scalar
original/rewrite items, not conditional, collection, or nested semantics; low agreement on
invalid-target rejection limits fallback claims. Primary experiments use one target and one
refresh, while a negative multi-referent stress test does not establish composition.

\paragraph{Causal attribution and external validity.}
The primary 20 template clusters and post-primary 40-state replication use temperature-zero calls
and do not characterize execution variance or all model families. The replication was frozen
after the mechanism hypothesis was selected and remains synthetic. The deterministic lock/reverify
controls expose the relevant policy extremes, but we have not rerun the named Binding Drift
Entity Lock and Independent Re-verification implementations on its workflow benchmark.
Compound-selector grounding remains a bottleneck. Fixed query-refresh order, local SQLite,
ToolSandbox-style tasks, and a two-cluster custom AppWorld case study do not establish
real-traffic prevalence. The public trace near-matches have no Stable/Flip contrast, the official
$\tau^3$ inventory has no strict opportunity, and the external adaptations have null conditional
TRI results; opportunity coverage and controller state
representation materially affect whether the phenomenon is observable.

\section{Conclusion}

World updates need not rebind targets. In the controlled benchmark, minimal
pre-refresh CTA has the highest or tied scalar point estimate, without a significant advantage
over typed variants. Typed lifecycle state exposes richer
policy but adds compilation errors; gates enforce only correct contracts. Composition and
external validity remain open. The supported design principle is narrow: decide whether a
description has become an identity commitment before incorporating later observations. The
corrected Generic audit and 240-task new-state replication confirm post-binding drift when the
initial ID is correct, while
the external ToolSandbox-style and AppWorld-backed studies find no such drift in the tested
full-history agents. Released AppWorld traces exhibit the underlying persistence structure, while
the $\tau^3$ audit shows that even a large dual-control suite need not contain a strict same-role
TRI opportunity. The result is
therefore a controller- and model-conditional mechanism diagnosis, not a universal LLM failure or
an autonomous-agent safety guarantee.
